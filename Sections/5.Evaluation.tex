The goal is to measure whether PAC and CA-ET reduce SSD I/O and improve throughput at comparable recall under equal memory budgets.

\section{Fairness Protocol: Memory-Budget Normalization}
\label{sec:fairness}
%-------------------------------------------------------------

The systems have different minimum memory requirements.
DiskANN and PaceANN mainly require the PQ-compressed vectors at startup; on SPACEV100M this is about 3.2\,GB.
Starling must additionally load layout metadata and its navigation graph, giving it a minimum RSS of about 3.07\,GB.
To compare equal hardware cost, we set a fixed total memory budget that includes PQ tables, metadata, and caches.
For the 100M experiments, this budget is 8\,GB, and all systems stay within this limit.

All measurements are cold-start runs.
The query set is processed once without warm-up or repetition, so PaceANN's cache can improve only through BFS seeding and demand fill during the measured run.
DiskANN and PaceANN use beam width $W=8$; Starling uses $W=4$, consistent with its original setting.

%-------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Measurement Methodology}
\label{sec:measurement}
%-------------------------------------------------------------

Concurrent serving is measured with a common TCP client that controls the number of in-flight queries $C$.
The client sends queries to each system's native server and measures QPS using wall-clock time.
Server-side latency is measured from the moment a worker receives a complete query packet until it sends the result, excluding TCP setup and transfer.

We report results at $C=16$ and $C=32$.
$C=16$ is the main comparison point because it is also PipeANN's fair operating point.
PipeANN's \texttt{pipe\_search} uses \texttt{io\_uring} SQPOLL and busy polling, so 16 search threads plus 16 SQPOLL threads fill the 32 logical CPU threads.
$C=32$ is used to show the behavior of the blocking-I/O systems under heavier concurrency.

The search-list length $L$ is swept across $\{20, 30, 50, 80, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300\}$ to produce recall--performance Pareto curves.
Systems are compared at comparable recall, following standard ANN benchmarking practice\cite{aumuller2020annbench}.

%-------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Metric Definitions}
\label{sec:metrics}
%-------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{description}
  \item[Recall@10]
    The fraction of returned top-10 results that appear in the ground-truth top-10 set, averaged over 10,000 queries.

  \item[QPS]
    Queries per second, measured by the client from the first query sent to the last response received.

  \item[Server-side latency]
    Mean, P50, P99, and P999 processing time measured inside the server. P99 and P999 are used to characterize tail latency.

  \item[MeanIOs]
    The average number of SSD reads issued per query, excluding cache hits.

  \item[RSS]
    Peak resident memory measured from \texttt{/proc/\$PID/status} using \texttt{VmHWM}.
\end{description}

\section{System Comparison at Iso-Recall}
\label{sec:eval-compare}
%-------------------------------------------------------------

Figures~\ref{fig:pareto-throughput} to~\ref{fig:pareto-io} compare DiskANN, Starling, PipeANN, and PaceANN at $C=16$ with an 8\,GB budget.
PaceANN uses OPQ at 32\,bytes/vector, beam width 8, PAC, and CA-ET.

On SPACEV100M, PaceANN leads all three baselines across the measured recall range.
On SIFT100M and DEEP100M, PaceANN consistently outperforms DiskANN and Starling, but PipeANN becomes stronger in the medium- and high-recall regions.
This shows that PaceANN's advantage is robust against DiskANN and Starling, but not universal against PipeANN.
Starling performs well at low recall because its page layout improves I/O density, but its advantage weakens as recall increases.

\section{Sensitivity to $\theta$}
\label{sec:eval-theta}
%-------------------------------------------------------------

The CA-ET threshold $\theta$ controls the tradeoff between saved I/O and recall.
Smaller values stop earlier; larger values are more conservative.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption[Effect of theta on recall and QPS]{Effect of $\theta$ on recall and QPS on SPACEV100M with $L=200$ and $C=16$}
\label{tab:eval-theta}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
$\theta$ & Recall@10 & QPS & Mean I/O \\
\midrule
1.0 & 0.8965 & 9045.3 & 39.78 \\
1.1 & 0.9543 & 6931.0 & 53.96 \\
1.2 & 0.9771 & 4350.1 & 89.15 \\
1.3 & 0.9801 & 3229.8 & 123.48 \\
1.4 & 0.9807 & 2790.8 & 144.26 \\
1.6 & 0.9808 & 2464.0 & 163.88 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The results show the expected tradeoff.
Very small $\theta$ values produce high QPS but lose recall.
For $\theta \ge 1.3$, recall is close to the no-early-termination baseline, while Mean I/O remains substantially below the baseline.
This thesis uses $\theta=1.3$ because it lies near the point where recall has saturated but early termination still saves I/O.
In practice, $\theta$ should be calibrated for each dataset because PQ quantization error affects the reliability of the stopping signal.
